How to be a product focussed engineer, and why it matters now more than ever

When code gets cheaper, judgment becomes more valuable.

Opening - 1 minute

Set the expectation: this is not a talk about becoming a product manager. It is about becoming a more effective engineer by understanding the world around the code.

The AI angle comes later. First establish that product focus has always mattered; AI makes the difference more visible and more urgent.

I use AI to write more and more of my code.

What value am I providing in the loop?

Personal setup - 2 minutes

Tell the personal story from the brain dump: after using AI prolifically for roughly two years, the model can now produce functional prototypes and substantial implementations very quickly.

Avoid defensiveness about whether AI is "really engineering." Use it as a sincere question about where your contribution moves when implementation becomes abundant.

What is your job as a software engineer?

Audience question - 1.5 minutes

Pause and let the room form answers: write code, solve technical problems, build systems, ship features, keep services reliable.

Acknowledge that these are real skills and responsibilities, but argue that they are means rather than the final outcome.

Outcome chain - 2 minutes

Most of us are not paid merely to produce code. We are paid because the code enables someone to do something that matters.

Walk from left to right. The further right you understand, the better the decisions you can make on the left.

The graph - 2.5 minutes

Use the memorable "up and to the right" idea, but broaden it beyond money. A company needs sustainable economic value; a school, nonprofit, or public institution may optimize for a different mission outcome.

Engineering metrics are proxies. Product focus means understanding how your proxy contributes to the outcome that actually matters.

Assumptions - 2.5 minutes

Engineers make product decisions even when they believe they are "just implementing the ticket." Requirements cannot contain every state, interaction, priority, or tradeoff.

The difference is whether those assumptions are informed by context or made accidentally.

Product focus is bringing the user's and organization's context into engineering decisions.

Definition - 2 minutes

Make clear that this is not about replacing product managers, designers, researchers, or domain experts. Building products is a team sport.

Product focus means knowing enough about the shared goal to make better local decisions and recognizing when you need to ask someone else.

Concrete story slot - 3 minutes

This slide needs one personal example. Choose a moment from logistics, education, developer tools, or a startup where the literal implementation would have been reasonable but user or business context changed the answer.

Structure the story as: request, hidden assumption, question asked, context discovered, decision changed, result.

Product focus recap - 1.5 minutes

Pause and consolidate the first half of the argument. Product focus is not a separate activity layered on top of engineering. It is the practice of connecting these four kinds of context while making engineering decisions.

The engineer does not need to personally own every area. The skill is knowing how they connect and when to bring in someone with deeper expertise.

AI makes implementation abundant.

It does not tell us which of the many possible implementations is worth having.

AI transition - 2 minutes

AI can turn a vague idea into a functional prototype remarkably quickly. Rewrites and alternatives are cheaper. That is real leverage.

But lowering the cost of answers increases the importance of asking the right question.

AI fills every gap with something plausible.

Plausible is useful. Plausible is not the same as correct.

The danger of plausible - 2.5 minutes

Agents are often getting better at proceeding rather than interrupting us with questions. We also put them in autonomous environments where they cannot ask questions even when they should.

If intent is vague, the output can be impressively complete and still be based on the wrong understanding of the problem.

Taste - 3 minutes

Taste can sound subjective. Define it as intuition about what actually makes sense in the current situation.

It is not simply personal preference. It is a synthesis of user understanding, domain knowledge, principles, experience, constraints, and feedback.

AI often gives us a strong version of the mean. Taste helps us decide which conventions fit, which should be challenged, and what may be correct but still wrong for this product.

Context bridge - 3 minutes

Agents will gain access to more organizational information. But access to information is not the same as knowing which information matters.

Much of the real product context is fragmented, outdated, contradictory, or held in people's heads. The engineer translates that context into something the agent can act on and evaluates the output against context the agent still could not see.

Make product context durable.

User scenarios and acceptance criteria
Tests that preserve important behavior
Design principles and interaction patterns
Examples, counterexamples, and quality bars
Architecture decisions and their rationale
Privacy, security, operational, and ethical constraints

Durable context - 2.5 minutes

These are not only documents for AI. They help people and agents make consistent decisions over time.

Taste cannot be captured completely in an instruction file, but examples, critique, principles, and repeated feedback make the quality bar more legible.

Mention that complexity still matters: AI can create unnecessary complexity faster, while humans still pay the cost of understanding, reviewing, operating, and trusting it.

AI section recap - 1.5 minutes

Bring the AI argument into one frame. More implementation capacity makes context, taste, and boundaries more consequential because each decision can now turn into working software much faster.

This is the answer to the opening question: these are increasingly important parts of the value the engineer provides in the loop.

User trust is part of the outcome. Measure responsibly: privacy, accessibility, security, incentives, and unintended harm all count.

Learning loop - 3 minutes

Engineers already believe in technical observability. Apply the same instinct to the product: are people using it, succeeding, abandoning it, or finding unexpected value?

Discuss the spectrum from telemetry and experiments to support requests, observation, interviews, and direct conversations. Each method reveals a different part of the truth.

The ability to measure behavior does not mean every form of measurement is appropriate. A local metric can improve while the actual product becomes more hostile.

Product focus is not "do anything that moves the graph." Sustainable value depends on preserving trust and understanding second-order effects.

Internal products - 2 minutes

Infrastructure and platform teams have customers too. The connection to the end user may be second-order, but the questions remain the same.

Faster builds, lower costs, better reliability, and more flexible platforms matter because they enable another team to create user value.

Ask five questions.

  1. Who is this for?
  2. What are they trying to accomplish?
  3. Why does that matter to the organization?
  4. How will we know this worked?
  5. Which user expectations, constraints, and existing behaviors must we preserve?

Practical takeaway - 2.5 minutes

Present these as questions students can ask in an internship and senior engineers can ask in a planning meeting tomorrow.

You do not need authority or a product title to ask for context. Curiosity is the starting behavior.

AI gives us more power to build. Product thinking helps us use that power on the right things.

The agent may write the implementation. The engineer connects it to the world in which it must succeed.

Close - 1 minute

Return to the opening question about your value in the loop. Your value is not just typing the implementation. It is the quality of the decisions you help the team make.
